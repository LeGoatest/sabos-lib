# Customer Pain and Solution Framing

> **Status:** Current SEObasic content/application guidance  
> **Scope:** Customer pain points, problem framing, PAS-style copy structure, service/landing-page messaging, and conversion-oriented content  
> **Knowledge type:** Industry copywriting pattern plus SEObasic application guidance; not a Google ranking rule or binding cross-domain contract  
> **Related contract:** [`../contracts/truth-and-evidence.md`](../contracts/truth-and-evidence.md)

Customer-focused content should begin from a real condition, friction, risk, unmet need, or desired outcome that the intended audience actually recognizes.

The purpose is not to manufacture anxiety. The purpose is to make the reader's situation clear, explain why it matters, and connect it to a credible solution.

## PAS: Problem → Agitate → Solution

A common copywriting pattern is **PAS**:

1. **Problem** — identify the customer's actual problem or unmet need.
2. **Agitate** — clarify the practical consequences, friction, cost, delay, risk, or frustration created by leaving the problem unresolved.
3. **Solution** — explain how the service, process, product, or recommended action addresses that problem.

SEObasic treats **agitate** as *clarifying truthful stakes*, not as permission for fear-based exaggeration.

When the word “agitate” encourages hype, use the equivalent framing:

```text
Problem → Stakes / Impact → Solution
```

For many service and landing pages, the stronger complete sequence is:

```text
Problem
→ Stakes / impact
→ Solution
→ Proof
→ Next action
```

## 1. Problem

State the situation in language the customer would recognize.

Good problem framing is:

- specific enough to be identifiable;
- directly related to the page's service or purpose;
- based on observable customer conditions rather than invented psychology;
- written in normal language rather than keyword variants;
- narrow enough that the page can actually resolve or explain it.

Examples of legitimate problem categories include:

- visible damage or deterioration;
- recurring maintenance issues;
- unfinished or delayed work;
- multiple small repairs accumulating at once;
- property turnover or preparation work;
- confusing project scope;
- difficulty coordinating several specialized providers;
- uncertainty about what needs to happen next.

Do not claim that every customer has the same pain point.

## 2. Agitate by explaining real stakes

The purpose of this step is to answer:

> **Why does this problem matter to the customer?**

Useful stakes can include:

- additional coordination;
- delays;
- disruption;
- recurring failures;
- avoidable rework;
- appearance or usability problems;
- uncertainty about scope or scheduling;
- genuine safety, compliance, financial, or property risks when those risks can be supported.

Do not:

- invent catastrophic outcomes;
- imply urgency that does not exist;
- turn a possibility into a certainty;
- use unsupported cost claims;
- manufacture fear around health, safety, finance, legal exposure, or property damage;
- repeatedly paraphrase the same consequence merely to make the page longer.

The [`Truth and Evidence Contract`](../contracts/truth-and-evidence.md) controls material claims.

## 3. Solution

The solution must answer the problem that was just presented.

Prefer concrete explanation over generic assurances.

Weak:

```text
We care about quality and provide solutions you can trust.
```

Stronger:

```text
We inspect the affected areas, define the work that needs to be completed,
review the scope with the customer, and schedule the approved repairs as one coordinated project.
```

A solution section can explain:

- what the service actually does;
- what is included;
- how the process works;
- who the service is appropriate for;
- available options;
- important limitations or prerequisites;
- expected next steps.

Do not use the solution section as a second version of the problem section.

## 4. Proof

Where proof exists, show evidence that supports the proposed solution.

Examples include:

- real project photographs;
- before-and-after documentation;
- case studies;
- process documentation;
- verified reviews or testimonials;
- relevant credentials when real and applicable;
- first-hand observations from completed work;
- clearly sourced technical or industry information.

Proof must remain truthful and attributable. Stock imagery, illustrative mockups, AI-generated examples, or hypothetical scenarios must not be presented as completed customer work.

## 5. Next action

After the reader understands the problem, solution, and available evidence, provide an appropriate next step.

Examples:

- request an estimate;
- schedule an assessment;
- call or text;
- view a related service;
- review a case study;
- check service availability;
- provide project details or photographs.

The call to action should fit the reader's stage rather than interrupt every paragraph.

## Customer-pain quality rules

### Use customer language without reducing the page to keyword repetition

Search-relevant terminology should emerge from accurate coverage of real problems, services, processes, materials, locations, and outcomes.

Do not create several paragraphs that restate the same pain point using slightly different keyword phrases.

See [`../websites/keyword-use.md`](../websites/keyword-use.md).

### One meaningful idea per section

If a section is about the problem, establish the problem.

If a section is about consequences, explain the consequences.

If a section is about the solution, explain the solution.

Avoid AI-style recursive prose where each paragraph merely rewords the previous paragraph without adding information.

### Match the pain to the service

A problem should not be included merely because it is emotionally persuasive.

If the business does not actually solve it, the page should not imply otherwise.

### Do not overstate commonality

Use language such as “can,” “may,” “often,” or a clearly scoped condition when outcomes vary.

Do not turn a possible consequence into “this will happen” unless the claim is supportable.

### Prefer useful depth over manufactured length

A page becomes comprehensive by covering the subject's real components, not by repeating its main phrase or customer problem.

Useful depth may include:

- problem types;
- service options;
- process;
- preparation;
- limitations;
- project examples;
- service availability;
- FAQs;
- evidence;
- next steps.

## Page-level application

A service or landing page can use the pattern without becoming a rigid copywriting template.

Example structure:

```text
Hero
  → desired outcome + immediate service clarity

Customer problem
  → what the visitor may be dealing with

Why it matters
  → practical impact / stakes

Service solution
  → what the business actually does

Process
  → how the work is approached

Proof
  → real projects / evidence / reviews

Questions / limitations
  → FAQs, prerequisites, timing, scope

CTA
  → appropriate next action
```

Not every page needs every step in this order. Informational pages, case studies, documentation, and navigational pages may require different structures.

## Relationship to people-first content and E-E-A-T

Customer-pain framing should serve the visitor first. It is not a technique for inserting more search terms.

A well-supported problem/solution page may also provide opportunities to demonstrate real experience, expertise, process transparency, authorship, and evidence. Those qualities can align with Google's E-E-A-T and people-first guidance, but PAS itself is not a Google ranking factor or Google requirement.

See [`../standards/google-eeat-and-helpful-content.md`](../standards/google-eeat-and-helpful-content.md).

## Review questions

Before publishing, ask:

```text
Is the stated problem real and relevant to this audience?
Are the consequences proportionate and supportable?
Does the proposed solution directly address the stated problem?
Did we add new information instead of repeatedly paraphrasing the same point?
Is proof clearly distinguishable from marketing claims?
Does the page explain enough for a reader to make a useful next decision?
Is the CTA appropriate to that decision?
```
